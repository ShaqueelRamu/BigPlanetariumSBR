What is the Milky Way?

We live inside a galaxy. Here's what that actually means.

Our home galaxy

The Milky Way is the galaxy that contains our solar system. When you look up at the night sky and see that faint pale band stretching across it — that's the Milky Way. You're looking at the disc of the galaxy edge-on, with billions of stars so far away they just blur together into a glow.

It's a barred spiral galaxy, which means it has a bright bar-shaped centre with spiral arms curling out from each end. Our solar system sits in one of those arms — the Orion Arm — about 26,000 light-years from the galactic centre. We're not in the middle, not on the outer edge, just somewhere fairly unremarkable in between.

The whole thing is about 100,000 light-years across and roughly 1,000 light-years thick at the disc. It's genuinely difficult to picture how big that is. If the Milky Way were the size of Europe, our entire solar system would be smaller than a full stop on this page.

Key facts

Shape

The Milky Way is a barred spiral galaxy. It has a dense central bar of older stars, with four main spiral arms winding outwards. The bar itself is about 27,000 light-years long. At the very centre is a supermassive black hole called Sagittarius A*, with a mass about 4 million times that of our Sun.

Size and scale

The disc is approximately 100,000 light-years in diameter and about 1,000 light-years thick. However there's also a much larger, roughly spherical halo of older stars and dark matter surrounding the disc, which extends for hundreds of thousands of light-years in every direction.

How many stars?

Estimates range from 100 billion to 400 billion stars. We can't count them directly — there's too much dust in the way and we're inside the thing — so the number is calculated from the galaxy's mass and the typical mass of stars. The true number is genuinely unknown.

Age

The Milky Way is approximately 13.6 billion years old, making it almost as old as the universe itself (which is about 13.8 billion years old). The oldest stars in the galaxy formed very shortly after the Big Bang, from the very first clouds of hydrogen and helium.

How fast is it moving?

Our solar system orbits the centre of the Milky Way at about 828,000 km/h. Even at that speed, one full orbit takes around 225–250 million years — known as a "cosmic year." Earth has completed about 20 of these since it formed.

Neighbouring galaxies

The Milky Way is part of a group of about 50 galaxies called the Local Group. Our nearest large neighbour is Andromeda, about 2.5 million light-years away. The two galaxies are actually on a collision course — they'll merge in about 4.5 billion years. Don't panic.

The black hole at the centre

At the very heart of the Milky Way sits Sagittarius A* (pronounced "Sagittarius A-star"), a supermassive black hole with a mass of about 4 million Suns. It's approximately 26,000 light-years from Earth, sitting in the direction of the constellation Sagittarius.

We can't see it directly — there's too much dust and gas in the way — but astronomers have tracked the orbits of stars right next to it for over 20 years. The stars move so fast and in such tight curves that something with enormous mass has to be there. In 2022, the Event Horizon Telescope project released the first actual image of Sagittarius A*, showing the glowing ring of material around it.

Despite being 4 million solar masses, Sagittarius A* is actually fairly quiet compared to the supermassive black holes in other galaxies. Some of those are billions of solar masses and actively pulling in huge amounts of material, making them visible across the universe as quasars.

Where are we in the galaxy?

The Milky Way has four main spiral arms: the Norma Arm, the Scutum–Centaurus Arm, the Carina–Sagittarius Arm, and the Perseus Arm. Our solar system is in a smaller, partial arm called the Orion Arm (or Orion Spur), which sits between the Perseus and Carina–Sagittarius arms.

Being in a spiral arm is actually pretty good for life. The arms contain the gas and dust clouds that form new stars — including our Sun. They're also not so densely packed that nearby supernovae would constantly bombard the solar system with radiation. We're in a fairly safe, relatively quiet neighbourhood. Cosmically speaking.

Perseus Arm
One of the two major arms, located further from the centre than us. Contains a lot of young, hot stars and star-forming regions.
Orion Arm (our location)
A partial arm where our solar system lives, about 26,000 light-years from the galactic centre. Smaller than the four main arms.
Carina–Sagittarius Arm
The arm closer to the centre than us. Contains several well-known nebulae including the Carina Nebula and the Lagoon Nebula.
Scutum–Centaurus Arm
One of the two major arms, connecting to one end of the central bar. Very rich in stars and star-forming regions.

What about dark matter?

Here's something that should genuinely keep you up at night: the stars, gas, and dust we can actually see in the Milky Way only account for about 5% of its total mass. The rest — roughly 95% — is dark matter, an unknown substance we can't see, detect directly, or identify. We only know it's there because the galaxy rotates in a way that can't be explained by the visible mass alone. The outer parts of the galaxy spin just as fast as the inner parts, which shouldn't happen without something extra holding it together. Whatever dark matter is, there's a lot of it, and we genuinely have no idea what it actually is.
