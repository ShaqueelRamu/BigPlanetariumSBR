The Planets

Eight worlds, billions of kilometres of space, and a whole lot of weirdness. Let's dig in.

Explore each planet

Mercury – The Speedy One

First planet from the Sun. Tiny, cratered, and absolutely scorching.

Mercury is the smallest planet in the solar system and the closest to the Sun, but it's not the hottest — that's Venus. Because Mercury has almost no atmosphere, it can't trap heat. Temperatures swing wildly: up to 430°C during the day and plummeting to −180°C at night.

Mercury orbits the Sun faster than any other planet — it completes one orbit in just 88 Earth days. But a day on Mercury (one full rotation) takes 59 Earth days. So a Mercury year is only about 1.5 Mercury days long. Completely bizarre.

💨 Did you know?

Mercury is shrinking. As its iron core cools and contracts, the surface is wrinkling like a dried-up apple. Scientists have found cliffs hundreds of kilometres long called "rupes" — essentially wrinkle ridges caused by the planet collapsing in on itself.

Venus – Earth's Evil Twin

Second planet from the Sun. Similar in size to Earth, but absolutely hellish.

Venus is almost exactly the same size as Earth, which is why people used to think it might be similar underneath those thick clouds. It is not. The surface is 465°C — hot enough to melt lead — with crushing atmospheric pressure 90 times that of Earth's. Early Soviet landers survived for about an hour before being destroyed.

Venus also rotates backwards compared to most planets, and so slowly that a Venusian day is longer than its year. If you could stand on the surface (you couldn't), you'd see the Sun rise in the west and set in the east.

🌡️ Did you know?

Venus is hotter than Mercury, even though it's further from the Sun. This is because of its extreme greenhouse effect — CO₂ traps so much heat that the temperature barely changes between day and night, or between the equator and the poles. It's basically a planet-wide oven.

Earth – Our Home

Third planet from the Sun. The only one we know for certain has life.

Earth is the largest of the four rocky planets and the only body in the universe confirmed to support life — though given the size of the universe, it would be pretty surprising if we were alone. What makes Earth special is the combination of liquid water, a protective magnetic field, a stable atmosphere, and just the right distance from the Sun.

Earth's Moon is unusually large relative to its host planet — it's thought to have formed when a Mars-sized body collided with early Earth about 4.5 billion years ago. The Moon stabilises Earth's axial tilt, which is a big part of why we have stable seasons and a consistent climate.

🌍 Did you know?

Earth is not a perfect sphere — it bulges at the equator because of its rotation. This means that if you measured from the centre of the Earth, the surface of Mount Chimborazo in Ecuador is actually further from the Earth's core than the summit of Mount Everest, even though Everest is taller above sea level.

Mars – The Red Planet

Fourth planet from the Sun. Reddish, rocky, and genuinely fascinating.

Mars has been capturing our imagination for centuries, and honestly for good reason. It's the most Earth-like planet in our solar system — it has seasons, polar ice caps, canyons, and even extinct volcanoes. It's cold though. Very cold. Surface temperatures average around −60°C, dropping to −125°C at the poles in winter. So… bring a coat.

One of the most exciting things about Mars right now is that we have actual robots on the surface. NASA's Perseverance rover has been roaming around since February 2021, drilling into rocks and looking for signs of ancient microbial life. And the Ingenuity helicopter became the first powered aircraft to fly on another planet. Just wild.

🤯 Did you know?

Olympus Mons on Mars is the tallest volcano in the entire solar system — about 22km high, nearly 3x the height of Mount Everest. It's so wide (600km across) that if you stood on the edge you wouldn't be able to see the other side because of the planet's curvature.

Jupiter – King of the Planets

Fifth planet from the Sun. The biggest planet in our solar system by a very long way.

Jupiter is enormous. You could fit 1,300 Earths inside it. It's a gas giant with a very turbulent atmosphere — those colourful bands are actually massive storm systems and wind patterns. Winds on Jupiter can reach 540 km/h.

The Great Red Spot is a storm bigger than Earth that has been raging for at least 350 years. Nobody knows exactly when it started — it was already there when we first got good enough telescopes to see it in the 1600s.

⚡ Did you know?

Jupiter acts as a kind of "cosmic vacuum cleaner" — its massive gravity attracts and absorbs asteroids and comets that would otherwise head towards the inner solar system. It's been protecting Earth for billions of years without any of us even knowing.

Saturn – Lord of the Rings

Sixth planet from the Sun. Famous for its rings, and even more impressive up close.

Saturn is a gas giant with no solid surface — just layers of gas getting denser as you go deeper. Its iconic ring system is made up of billions of pieces of ice and rock, ranging from tiny grains to chunks the size of a house.

One genuinely weird fact: Saturn is less dense than water. If you could find a bathtub big enough, Saturn would float in it. It's made mostly of hydrogen and helium, just like the Sun.

Saturn's moon Enceladus shoots geysers of water ice into space from its south pole. Scientists think there's a liquid water ocean beneath the icy surface — making it one of the best candidates for extraterrestrial life in our solar system.

Uranus – The Sideways Planet

Seventh planet from the Sun. An ice giant that orbits completely on its side.

Uranus is an ice giant — its interior is made mostly of water, methane, and ammonia ices rather than gas like Jupiter or Saturn. Its striking blue-green colour comes from methane in its atmosphere, which absorbs red light and reflects blue and green.

The most unusual thing about Uranus is its tilt — it's tilted 98 degrees on its side, so it essentially rolls around the Sun like a bowling ball. One pole spends about 42 years in constant sunlight, then 42 years in complete darkness. This is thought to be caused by a massive collision early in the solar system's history.

🫧 Did you know?

Uranus is the coldest planet in the solar system, even though Neptune is further from the Sun. Scientists aren't entirely sure why — it's thought that Uranus has very little internal heat escaping from its core, unlike every other planet. It's essentially cold all the way through.

Neptune – The Windy One

Eighth and furthest planet from the Sun. Dark, cold, and home to the fastest winds in the solar system.

Neptune is the most distant planet in our solar system — so far away that sunlight takes over four hours to reach it. Despite this, it has the strongest winds of any planet, reaching up to 2,100 km/h. Nobody is entirely sure where the energy comes from given how little sunlight it receives.

Neptune was the first planet to be predicted mathematically before it was actually observed. Astronomers noticed irregularities in Uranus's orbit and calculated that another planet must be causing them — and when they pointed a telescope at the predicted location in 1846, there it was.

🌊 Did you know?

Neptune's largest moon, Triton, orbits the planet backwards — in the opposite direction to Neptune's rotation. This strongly suggests it was captured from the Kuiper Belt rather than forming alongside Neptune. Triton is also slowly spiralling inward and will eventually be torn apart by Neptune's gravity in about 3.6 billion years.
